Advanced Thermal Management Technology Using Nanocomposites

Seunghyun Baik*

  • School of Mechanical Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, 16419, Korea

Thermally conductive polymer-matrix nanocomposites have received considerable attention for the advanced thermal management of electronic devices. The thermal interface material (TIM) fills the inevitable gap between the heat source and heat exchanger to decrease contact thermal resistance (Rc). Our first TIM research employed silver (Ag) flakes and multi-walled carbon nanotubes embellished with Ag nanoparticles (nAgMWNTs) in rigid epoxy matrix [1]. The 1-dimensional long nanotubes constructed thermal transport bridges between Ag flake islands, and the contact interface was improved by the coalescence between Ag flakes and Ag nanoparticles pre-functionalized on the surface of nanotubes. This approach provided a very high thermal conductivity (κ) of ~160 W m-1 K-1 as an adhesive type TIM [1]. However, the total thermal resistance (Rt) was large as a pad-type TIM due to the rigid epoxy matrix. We realized that there was a trade-off behavior between κ and Rc with regard to hardness [2]. The research efforts to overcome this trade-off behavior will be discussed including sandwich-structure [2] and healable phase-change TIMs [3]. The TIMs with non-oxidized copper nanoparticles and nanosatellite particles will also be introduced [4, 5]. The tunneling transport between nanosatellite particles dramatically enhances both electrical and thermal transport properties [6-9]. The recent progress in our laboratory will also be introduced.

Quantum Engineering with Trapped Ions

Junki Kim1*

  • 1Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea

QuETI Lab (Quantum Engineering with Trapped Ions) focuses on developing trapped-ion qubit platforms toward scalable and practical quantum information processing. Trapped ions offer exceptionally long coherence times and high-precision quantum control, making them a promising platform for quantum computing. However, scaling trapped-ion systems remains challenging due to technical limitations in optical control, trap fabrication, and system architecture. In this talk, I will introduce our ongoing research efforts to address these challenges and discuss potential directions for future research and collaboration. In the first part, I will present our experimental efforts to develop trapped-ion quantum computing platforms. We have constructed two cryogenic ion-trap systems and successfully trapped ytterbium and barium ions, respectively. I will highlight several technologies we have been developing along the way, including three-dimensional microfabricated ion traps, optically-phase-locked continuous-wave Raman laser systems, and compact cryogenic RF resonators. In the second part, I will discuss our recent architectural study on scaling trapped-ion quantum computers through ion shuttling. We propose a shuttling-based distributed quantum computing architecture in which entanglement is distributed by physically transporting qubits between processing modules. We evaluate its system-level performance and compare it with two representative approaches: the quantum charge-coupled device (QCCD) architecture and photonic distributed quantum computing. By presenting our research from both experimental and architectural perspectives, I hope to stimulate discussions on shared research interests and potential opportunities for collaboration.

  1. Seunghyun Baek, Seok-Hyung Lee, Dongmoon Min, and Junki Kim, arXiv:2512.02890

Quantum Materials Research in the Department of Physics at SKKU

Youngkuk Kim

  • Department of Physics, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Republic of Korea

The Department of Physics at Sungkyunkwan University brings together complementary approaches to quantum materials, spanning epitaxial growth, spectroscopy, and theoretical design. This talk will introduce four participating research groups and highlight their research activities.

Woo Seok Choi’s Epitaxial Complex Oxide Lab investigates the growth and physical properties of transition-metal oxide heterostructures. Combining pulsed laser epitaxy with optical spectroscopy, the group explores electronic and magnetic behavior in engineered oxide structures, connecting controlled synthesis with the characterization of emergent phenomena and functional properties.

Youngkuk Kim combines first-principles calculations with effective theories to investigate quantum materials. His research examines how symmetry, topology, quantum geometry, and electronic correlations govern novel phases, with interests including flat bands in kagome and heavy fermion systems, low-dimensional materials, and the control of electronic states by light and electric fields.

Hoon Kim investigates correlated materials through optical and X-ray spectroscopy, including Raman scattering and resonant inelastic X-ray scattering. His group uses pump–probe methods to study dynamical responses and their control, with particular interests in iridates, ruthenates, and routes to engineered quantum states through periodic driving and cavity environments.

Byungmin Sohn combines in situ oxide molecular beam epitaxy with angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy to investigate the electronic structure of low-dimensional correlated oxides. His research connects precisely controlled growth with momentum-resolved measurements, addressing how orbital selectivity, electronic correlations, and reduced dimensionality give rise to Mott transitions and flat bands.

Together, these research directions offer opportunities for SKKU⁠–⁠Rice collaboration linking materials synthesis, experimental characterization, and theoretical understanding to explore and control quantum states in solids.

Predicting Quantum Decoherence in Solid-State Spin Qubits: From Atomistic Defects to Quantum Devices

Hosung Seo1,*

  • 1Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea

Spin qubits in solids, such as the nitrogen-vacancy (NV) center in diamond, are leading platforms for quantum sensing and quantum networks. Their performance is limited by decoherence: the qubit loses quantum information through magnetic interactions with surrounding defects and impurities that form a fluctuating “spin bath.” Understanding which defects cause decoherence, and how, is key to engineering better quantum materials. Our group develops theoretical and computational approaches to quantum materials and solid-state qubits, combining first-principles electronic-structure calculations, quantum many-body spin-dynamics simulations, and close comparison with experiment to predict defect properties and qubit coherence from the atomic scale. In this talk, I will present a predictive framework for decoherence of NV ensembles in diamond (Fig. 1) [1]. Instead of approximating real defect populations as a single homogeneous bath, we build defect-resolved spin Hamiltonians for nitrogen-, vacancy-, and hydrogen-related defects. We find that coherence depends not only on defect density but also on defect identity and bath composition: mixed defect populations can suppress or enhance decoherence. Our calculations reproduce magnetic-field-dependent Hahn-echo measurements across defect concentrations and identify vacancy-related defects as critical contributors beyond the conventional nitrogen (P1) bath. I will close by discussing opportunities for SKKU⁠–⁠Rice collaboration on qubit materials, defect engineering, and quantum sensing.

Cryogenic RF and Microwave Research for Quantum Computing

Sang-Hee Shin1*, Manoj Stanley2

  • 1Department of Quantum Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea
  • 2National Physical Laboratory, Teddington, U.K.

Progress in quantum computing depends on a thorough understanding of RF and microwave component performance at cryogenic temperatures, including the tens-of-millikelvin regime. However, commercial components are typically not specified for operation at these temperatures, leaving a substantial gap in performance data. Accurate in-situ characterization is further complicated by the long cables connecting room-temperature instruments, such as vector network analyzers (VNAs), to cryogenic devices, which introduce signal loss and noise that limit measurement accuracy. This presentation describes cryogenic S-parameter measurement capabilities developed to address these challenges, focusing on a remote calibration and measurement unit for connectorized coaxial devices at the millikelvin stage [1,2]. It also highlights ongoing developments in on-wafer cryogenic measurements, including a custom calibration substrate designed for accurate characterization at temperatures down to 4 K. The talk will provide an overview of the measurement methodologies, previous work, and current progress in developing these systems.

An Ab-initio Workflow from Defect Thermodynamics to Spin Bath Decoherence: Assessing MgO as a Quantum Host Material

Yonghyun Kwon1, Taejoon Park2, and Hosung Seo1,2,*

  • 1Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Korea
  • 2SKKU Advanced Institute of Nanotechnology (SAINT), Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Korea

There is growing interest in finding defect-based spin qubits with long coherence times in industrially mature, CMOS-compatible hosts. A standard criterion for selecting a host is a low nuclear spin concentration, which provides a magnetically quiet environment [1]. However, electron spin baths from paramagnetic defects have rarely been considered, because assessing them requires knowing which defects form and at what concentrations.

Here, we develop an ab-initio framework combining hybrid density functional theory [2] and cluster-correlation expansion (CCE) theory [3] to link defect thermodynamics to qubit decoherence. Using MgO as a model host, we evaluate the electron spin bath arising from common intrinsic defects and unintentional transition-metal (TM) impurities under experimentally relevant thermodynamic conditions. We find that intrinsic defects are too scarce to appreciably limit coherence. TM impurities, by contrast, incorporate at low energy cost, and their electron spin bath dominates spin decoherence above sub-ppm concentrations. Thus, in MgO, TM impurities rather than the dilute nuclear spin bath can set the coherence time. Our framework is general and provides a new tool for assessing the magnetic noise environment of a wide range of candidate host materials.

  3. W. Yang and R.-B. Liu, Phys. Rev. B, 78, 085315 (2008).

Poster P02 · Poster session

Tensor Network Compression of Cluster Dynamics within the Cluster-Correlation Expansion for Many-Qubit Spin Register Coherence

Huijin Park1,2†, Seongpyo Hong3†, Junha Park4†, Junghyun Lee3,5*, and Hosung Seo1,3,4*

  • 1SKKU Advanced Institute of Nanotechnology, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Korea
  • 2Pritzker School of Molecular Engineering, University of Chicago, Chicago, IL, USA
  • 3Center for Quantum Information, Korea Institute of Science and Technology, Seoul, Korea
  • 4Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Korea
  • 5Division of Nano and Information Technology, KIST School, Korea University of Science and Technology, Seoul, Korea

Scaling solid-state spin registers to many qubits is important for quantum computation, communication, and sensing, and requires a quantitative understanding of multi-qubit decoherence in complex spin environments [1]. While first-principles approaches such as the cluster-correlation expansion (CCE) and its generalized form (gCCE) have successfully described single-qubit decoherence, their application to multi-qubit dynamics remains limited by the exponential growth of the Hilbert space. Tensor network (TN) methods can mitigate this growth, but long-range interactions in realistic spin baths limit their efficiency. Here, we introduce TN-gCCE, in which the cluster expansion factorizes the bath contribution into cluster correlations, while TNs compress the quantum state of each cluster into a matrix product state (MPS) according to its entanglement structure. We apply TN-gCCE to multi–nitrogen-vacancy (NV) center spin registers coupled to 13C nuclear-spin baths, a canonical model system. For up to six NV centers, TN-gCCE shows excellent agreement with gCCE under Ramsey and Hahn-echo sequences. For six-NV Hahn-echo simulations, it also reduces the computational cost about 13fold and the peak memory per core about 8fold. Our results establish TN-gCCE as a powerful and scalable framework for modeling decoherence in solid-state spin-qubit registers, providing a pathway toward the design and optimization of large-scale quantum systems.

  1. C. L. Degen et al., Rev. Mod. Phys. 89, 035002 (2017).

Toward AI-Assisted Microstrip Filter Synthesis

Seungcheon Shin1,* and Sanghee Shin1

  • 1Department of Quantum Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Republic of Korea

We are developing a simulation-based workflow for compact microstrip filters as a step toward AI-assisted design [1]. Pixel-based band-pass filter design using a genetic algorithm has been reported [2]. The present work explores via-free band-pass formation over 1-18 GHz using a 4 × 4 mm design region discretized into 40 × 40 binary cells. A deterministic rule narrows selected gaps to 25 µm while preserving metal connectivity. Starting from six parents selected from an 8,000-layout dataset, a mutation-based evolutionary search generates coupled-line and pixel modifications. MATLAB method-of-moments simulations evaluate each candidate, and selection considers transmission requirements and passband matching. The search evaluated 1,507 unique layouts and identified 147 band-pass-like candidates that passed a numerical passivity check; none met all target specifications. Candidate #1524 achieved a minimum simulated insertion loss of 1.6 dB at 12 GHz in MATLAB (Fig. 1). An HFSS cross-check reproduced the transmission notch near 8.7 GHz, with a mean absolute S21 difference of 4.0 dB over 1-18 GHz. Differences in matching and upper-stopband rejection remain unresolved. Current efforts focus on improving passband flatness and reconciling the solvers. Surrogate and diffusion-model training, fabrication, and room-temperature and cryogenic measurements are planned as subsequent steps.

Cryogenic RF Cable for Quantum Computing

Seonyong Kim1, Sang-Hee Shin1,*

  • 1Department of Quantum Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea

Scalable superconducting quantum computing requires RF interconnects that combine broadband electrical performance with compact packaging and low thermal loading. We present ongoing development of flexible RF cables and passive components for cryogenic quantum hardware. We are developing polyimide-based cables using silver (Ag) or niobium–titanium (NbTi) conductors, together with PCB transitions and copper clamp mounts. Direct-connected microstrip and stripline prototypes have been fabricated. Room-temperature measurements of an Ag microstrip through-line show an insertion loss below 2.5 dB and a return loss above 11 dB over 1–20 GHz (Fig. 1). Building on these initial measurements, the next step is to measure the thru, line 1, line 2, and reflect standards and perform thru-reflect-line (TRL) calibration under cryogenic conditions. In parallel, electromagnetic simulation of a two-channel cable has been completed, while fabrication remains a next-stage task. Our longer-term goal is to develop multichannel flexible cables with integrated filters and attenuators, targeting increased channel density and reduced heat load. Our test infrastructure includes a room-temperature RF probe station and a dilution refrigerator for cryogenic hardware evaluation. Further work will pursue the integration of transmission lines, passive components, and packaging toward compact interconnect solutions for superconducting quantum systems.

Poster P05 · Poster session

Soft-Information Decoding Using Analog Readout Signals of Superconducting Qubits

Soo-Seong Kim1, Yu-Ha Choi2, Seok-Hyung Lee1,3,*

  • 1Department of Quantum Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Republic of Korea
  • 2Elmore Family School of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana 47907, USA
  • 3Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Republic of Korea

Reliable large-scale quantum computation requires quantum error correction (QEC). The surface code corrects errors by decoding syndromes obtained from repeated stabilizer measurements [1]. In superconducting qubits, readout yields continuous in-phase and quadrature (IQ) signals, but conventional decoding uses only their binary outcomes, discarding the confidence of individual measurements (Fig. 1). Since readout errors are a major noise source, this information loss can limit QEC performance [2].

Soft decoding incorporates this analog readout information into decoding weights. Previous studies mainly investigated soft decoding in memory experiments, which evaluate the preservation of logical information over time [3–5]. In contrast, stability experiments assess the reliability of inferring the global product of stabilizer measurement outcomes across a spatially extended region, a benchmark relevant to logical measurements in lattice surgery [6]. Although soft decoding also benefits stability experiments [7], how much analog readout information improves logical-measurement reliability and reduces measurement overhead, and which error mechanisms limit these gains, remain to be quantified. As an initial step, we simulated memory and stability experiments under a common homogeneous circuit-level noise model with Gaussian IQ readout, and compared hard and soft minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM) decoders with the same readout-error calibration.

At the largest settings studied (memory: d = 17; stability: R = 17), soft decoding reduced logical error rates (LERs) by factors of 2.35 and 20.84, respectively (Fig. 2). In future work, we will investigate how readout misclassification (soft flips) and pre-measurement bit-flip errors (hard flips) affect these gains, how spatially inhomogeneous readout noise influences them, and whether similar improvements can be achieved for logical measurements performed by lattice surgery.

Poster P06 · Poster session

Efficient Post-Selection for General Quantum LDPC Codes

GyuHyeong Choi3 (presenter), reviewing work by Seok-Hyung Lee1,2, Lucas H. English1, and Stephen D. Bartlett1

  • 1Centre for Engineered Quantum Systems, School of Physics, The University of Sydney, Sydney, New South Wales 2006, Australia
  • 2Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Republic of Korea
  • 3Department of Quantum Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University

Post-selection improves the reliability of quantum computation by discarding low-confidence runs, at the cost of a reduced acceptance rate. Existing approaches based on the logical gap can become computationally expensive as the number of logical qubits increases. This poster reviews efficient post-selection methods developed by Lee, English, and Bartlett [1] for quantum low-density parity-check codes, using cluster-based metrics obtained from a single decoding run. Numerical simulations with circuit-level noise demonstrate substantial reductions in the logical error rate of accepted runs. For a 12-round memory experiment with the [[144,12,12]] bivariate bicycle code at a physical error rate of 0.1%, the method achieves an approximately 1,000-fold reduction while aborting about 1% of runs. The approach also extends to real-time decoding using a sliding window, enabling early abort decisions during circuit execution.

  1. S.-H. Lee, L. H. English, and S. D. Bartlett, “Efficient Post-Selection for General Quantum LDPC Codes,” arXiv:2510.05795 (2025).

Poster P07 · Poster session

CMOS ICs for Scalable Quantum Computing and Intelligent RF Systems

Yeonseo Choi1, Juntaek Oh1,*

  • 1Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea

Scaling superconducting-qubit quantum computers to thousands of qubits and beyond requires the high-frequency electronics that control and read out each qubit to be densely integrated close to the cryogenic environment. Today, controlling and measuring qubits relies on a large set of room-temperature instruments, such as microwave signal generators, arbitrary waveform generators, and digitizers, together with bulky cryogenic components such as ferrite circulators and isolators, attenuators, and filters. These are connected by numerous coaxial cables that run from room temperature down to the millikelvin stage. As the number of qubits grows, this architecture fundamentally limits scalability in terms of heat load, wiring complexity, system size, and cost. Our group, the Integrated Circuits and Systems Laboratory (ICSL), conducts research on silicon CMOS integrated circuits for Cryo-CMOS SoCs for scalable quantum computing. To minimize the equipment, cabling, and footprint required for qubit measurement, we design circuits that operate inside the cryogenic refrigerator and replace these room-temperature instruments and bulky components with compact ICs. Our work includes Cryo-CMOS readout and control ICs, covering the key building blocks of the receiver (Rx) chain, such as low-noise amplifiers (LNAs), mixers, and baseband amplifiers [1]. We also develop CMOS circulators and isolators that replace ferrite-based components, circulator-integrated readout receivers, cryogenic quantum RF metrology SoCs, and cryogenic interconnections. In this poster, I will introduce our research on CMOS SoCs for scalable quantum computing, including Cryo-CMOS readout and control ICs and a fully integrated CMOS readout receiver IC. In particular, I will focus on the CMOS LNA, the first stage of the CMOS readout receiver. The proposed LNA, implemented in a 65-nm CMOS process, uses a three-coil coupled transformer to achieve wideband performance covering the qubit readout band.

Poster P08 · Poster session

Cryo-compatible objective and sCMOS camera readout for individual state detection of trapped ions in a 4 K trap

Dongyeon Lee1, Sungjoo Lim2, Junki Kim1,2*

  • 1Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea
  • 2SKKU Advanced Institute of Nanotechnology (SAINT), Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea

Trapped ions are a leading platform for quantum computing, with each ion storing a qubit in two internal states. The qubit state is measured by state-dependent fluorescence: under resonant laser light, an ion in one state scatters many photons (bright), while an ion in the other scatters almost none (dark). Detecting the state of each ion individually in a multi-ion chain requires high collection efficiency and low crosstalk between neighboring ions. In a cryogenic trap, this must be achieved through a vacuum window, from a long working distance, and despite thermal contraction on cooling. We develop individual state detection for 171Yb+ ions in a 4 K trap by combining a cryo-compatible objective lens with sCMOS camera readout. The single aspheric objective (NA 0.6, 10 mm working distance) confines 90% of an ion’s light within a 2.2 µm square with ~0.1% crosstalk at 5 µm spacing, and gives a single-ion SPAM error of 0.73% with PMT detection. The sCMOS camera is integrated into the experimental control system to read out each ion’s state. Because camera pixel noise dominates, we characterize the counts and the sCMOS SPAM error versus the size of the region of interest (ROI), where a larger ROI gathers more light but adds more pixel noise.

Poster P09 · Poster session

From Fridge to Browser: Full-Stack Remote Access to an In-Lab Superconducting QPU

Dong-Wan Kim1, Youngdu Kim2, Seungwook Woo2, Junyeol Im1, Jongwon Park1, Youngwoo Ko1, Junkyo Kim1, Yonuk Chong1,2,3,*

  • 1Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Korea
  • 2SKKU Advanced Institute of Nano Technology (SAINT), Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Korea
  • 3Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Korea

Superconducting qubits are a leading platform for quantum computing. Yet in many labs, a working qubit device is not quite a usable computer. Every algorithm still requires hand-written pulse-level experiment code, and no one outside the lab can reach the device. Our lab designs, fabricates, and characterizes superconducting qubits in-house, using our own quantum fab and dilution fridge. What was missing was the last step, from a measured chip to a QPU that others can use. This led us to develop Orbit (qpu.skku.edu), a cloud platform that connects chips at a few millikelvin to the user’s browser through RF control electronics and a lab server (Fig. 1a). The typical workflow of a program on a quantum computer has three steps. A user composes abstract quantum gates into a quantum circuit, chooses a quantum device, and runs the program to get the results. Orbit shows the user all five layers that a program written in a gate composer, OpenQASM [1], or Qiskit goes through in order (Fig. 1b). The program is confirmed as a valid quantum circuit, transpiled onto our chip’s qubits and native gates, compiled into pulse sequences, and executed over many shots. Orbit also supports state tomography and VQE, which we ran for H2 on our own chip. Orbit has been tested in public through four demos in 2025–2026 on 4- and 6-qubit chips, serving 712 visitors in total.

  1. A. Cross et al., ACM Trans. Quantum Comput., 3(3), 1–50 (2022).

Poster P10 · Poster session

Efficient Optimization of Superconducting NbN Thin Film for SNSPD and Quantum Applications

Seongheon Song1, Dongki Cho1, Beomgyu Choi2, Seungwook Woo2, Youngdu Kim2, Jongwon Park1, Youngwoo Ko1, Junyeol Im1, Junkyo Kim1 and Yonuk Chong1,2,3*

  • 1Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Korea
  • 2SKKU Advanced Institute of Nano Technology (SAINT), Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Korea
  • 3Department of Nano Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Korea

Niobium nitride (NbN) is a widely used material in superconducting quantum devices, such as superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors (SNSPDs) and superconducting qubits, with the film thickness varied to each application. However, the properties of NbN films depend strongly on deposition conditions, making systematic process optimization essential. In this work, we report the optimization of deposition parameters for NbN films grown by RF-biased reactive DC magnetron sputtering. The target discharge current and substrate RF bias were tuned using the superconducting critical temperature Tc as the figure of merit. To optimize film quality, we analyzed how superconductivity correlates with film properties, including crystal structure and resistivity. With the optimized conditions, a nanobridge SNSPD fabricated from the ultrathin film exhibited internal detection efficiency saturation, suggesting the applicability of the proposed optimization workflow to superconducting devices.

Superconducting Quantum Devices based on Josephson Junction – Qubits and Parametric Amplifiers

The Josephson junction, a dissipationless nonlinear inductive element based on Cooper-pair tunneling, is a fundamental building block of various superconducting circuits, including qubits and parametric amplifiers (Fig. 1). We demonstrate transmon qubits composed of niobium electrodes and aluminum Josephson junctions on a silicon substrate, exhibiting energy relaxation times (T1) of up to 582 μs and Hahn-echo coherence times (T2E) of up to 801 μs. We further extended this process to a 54-qubit heavy-hex processor in a fully planar layout with airbridge crossovers, and characterized its single- and two-qubit gates. The Josephson traveling-wave parametric amplifier (JTWPA) is widely used as the first-stage amplifier for qubit readout, as it provides near-quantum-limited, high-gain amplification over a wide instantaneous bandwidth. In this work, we designed and fabricated JTWPAs, and analyzed their wafer-scale yield and performance. Of the 33 devices fabricated on a single wafer, 15 were excluded during visual pre-screening due to unintended shorts or opens. We measured the remaining 18 JTWPAs, 17 of which showed gain exceeding 18 dB across the 6.5-7.5 GHz band used for qubit readout. This corresponds to a yield of 94% among the measured devices, indicating that visual inspection is effective for identifying the working devices.

Simulation-Based Parameter Optimization of Superconducting Transmon Qubit

Jisu Shin1, Yujin Won1, Junkyo Kim1, Junhwan Lee2, and Yonuk Chong1,3*

  • 1Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Korea.
  • 2Department of Advanced Materials Science and Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, 16419, Korea.
  • 3SKKU Advanced Institute of Nano Technology (SAINT) and Department of Nano Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon 16419, Korea.

Superconducting transmon qubits, a leading platform for quantum computing, rely on a Josephson junction to provide the anharmonicity that isolates the two lowest energy levels as a qubit. Their performance depends on a careful balance: the ratio of Josephson to charging energy, EJ/EC, must be large enough to suppress charge noise yet small enough to retain sufficient anharmonicity [1]. Because both energies are set by the physical layout, designing a qubit means translating target Hamiltonian parameters into device geometry. This work was carried out by undergraduate researchers in an experimental superconducting quantum circuits group at SKKU, using the same electromagnetic simulation tools employed in research-grade qubit design: Quantum Metal, Ansys Q3D, and Ansys HFSS. In this poster, we present the simulation-based design of a single transmon qubit coupled to a readout resonator. Instead of drawing a geometry and reading off its parameters, we first set target values of 5 GHz and −300 MHz for the qubit frequency and anharmonicity, iterated the layout toward them. Capacitances extracted with Q3D were converted into Hamiltonian parameters by lumped oscillator model (LOM) analysis, and tuning the junction inductance, junction capacitance, and pad geometry converged the design to 5.005 GHz, −301 MHz, and EJ/EC=50.6. HFSS eigenmode simulation then placed the readout resonator at 7.266 GHz, a 2.26 GHz detuning suitable for dispersive readout [2].

Observation of a Topological Edge State in the Anisotropic Quantum Rabi Model and Prospects for Dissipative Quantum Simulation on a Trapped-Ion Platform

Sungjoo Lim1, Christopher G. Yale2, Brian K. McFarland2, Edward C. Tortorici2, Daniel S. Lobser2, Melissa C. Revelle2, Susan M. Clark2, Mahn-Soo Choi3, Taehee Kim1, Seunghyun Baek1, Chanyang Im1, Dongyeon Lee1, Baesung Park1, Junki Kim4,*

  • 1SAINT, SKKU Advanced Institute of Nanotechnology & Department of Nano Science and Technology, Sungkyunkwan University
  • 2Sandia National Laboratories, Albuquerque, New Mexico 87123, USA
  • 3Department of Physics, Korea University, Seoul 02841, Republic of Korea
  • 4Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea

Trapped ions are a well-established platform for quantum simulation, offering precise control over both internal and motional degrees of freedom. Their internal spin states and quantized motion naturally represent spin and bosonic modes, making trapped ions well suited to simulating spin–boson systems. Independent control of the rotating and counter-rotating interactions further allows a wide range of spin–boson couplings to be realized experimentally. We investigate the anisotropic quantum Rabi model (AQRM), in which the coupled spin–motional states form a synthetic lattice that supports topological phenomena [1]. Both edge and bulk states in this model are localized, so identifying the topological edge state requires signatures beyond localization. We adiabatically prepare the eigenstates and measure both the spin and motional degrees of freedom, identifying the edge state through its chirality and near-complete spin–motion separability. The bosonic component of the edge state is squeezed, and phase-space measurements reveal up to 6.45 dB of squeezing. Controlling dissipation alongside coherent interactions would further broaden the scope of spin–boson quantum simulation. We are developing an optical system in which acousto-optic deflectors (AODs) steer the Raman beams and an optical-pumping beam is added downstream for state initialization. Combining this dissipative optical pumping with coherent Raman control will lay the groundwork for engineered dissipation and open-system quantum simulation.

Coherent Control of a 171Yb+ Ion Using Independent Continuous-Wave Raman Lasers with an Optical Phase-Locked Loop

Taehee Kim1,2, Sungjoo Lim1,2, Seunghyun Baek1,2, Chanyang Im1,2, Dongyeon Lee1,2, Baesung Park1,2, Junki Kim1,2,3,*

  • 1SKKU Advanced Institute of Nanotechnology (SAINT), Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea
  • 2Department of Nano Science and Technology, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea
  • 3Department of Quantum Information Engineering, Sungkyunkwan University, Suwon, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea

In hyperfine qubits such as 171Yb+, two laser beams that differ in frequency by the qubit splitting drive stimulated Raman transitions, and their beat-note phase sets the phase of each qubit operation. Mode-locked lasers, a common Raman source for 171Yb+, require length-matched beam paths so that their pulses overlap at the ion, whereas continuous-wave (CW) beams do not. In this poster, we present a 171Yb+ Raman source built from two independent CW lasers locked by an optical phase-locked loop (OPLL) (Fig. 1). Two 1109 nm fiber lasers are phase-locked at a 4.214 GHz offset before frequency tripling, so the 370 nm beams differ by the 12.643 GHz qubit frequency. The locked beat note has an in-loop RMS phase error of 78.5 mrad from 1 Hz to 10 MHz. Because the beams are detuned only 0.46 THz from the 2P1/2 level, co-propagating beams with less than 100 μW each drive Rabi oscillations at 35.6 kHz, with a differential light shift of 2.7% of this Rabi frequency. Raman Ramsey interferometry gives a coherence time of 302 ± 58 ms, so the phase relation set at 1109 nm is preserved through tripling and UV delivery for more than 104 Rabi periods. Counter-propagating beams resolve radial motional sidebands and sideband-cool one mode to a mean phonon number of 0.042(9).
